Should you raise one unit or both units?

Discrimination-risk analysis under the federal Fair Housing Act and California law, and the recommended uniform approach so any increase withstands review by a San Francisco tenant lawyer.

The short answer

Raise both units under the same documented process — it is the lowest-risk approach

Applying the identical annual allowable increase (1.6%, currently) to both rent-controlled units, with proper license and notice for each, treats similarly situated tenants consistently and best supports a finding that the increase was reasonable, normal business practice, and non-discriminatory.

When raising only one unit can be lawful

A landlord may increase rent on one unit but not the other if the difference is driven by legitimate, unit-specific, non-protected factors, for example:

  • Different anniversary dates (each unit's 12-month window falls on a different date);
  • Different base rents, so the same percentage produces different dollar amounts;
  • Different tenancy start dates or banked-increase histories;
  • An increase on one unit approved through a Rent Board petition (e.g., capital-improvement passthrough) that does not apply to the other.

Documentation matters. The reason for treating the units differently must be written down and tied to verifiable facts (lease dates, base rents, Rent Board filings), not to who the tenants are.

Disparate treatment vs. disparate impact

  • Disparate treatment: intentionally increasing rent for one tenant because of a protected characteristic — unlawful.
  • Disparate impact: a neutral policy that disproportionately harms a protected group can still create liability — another reason to apply the same percentage and process to both units and keep records.

Recommended approach for your two units

  1. Apply the same documented annual allowable increase (1.6%) to both Unit 1 and Unit 2.
  2. Serve each tenant their own compliant written notice (dollar amount, percentage, effective date; at least 30 days; +5 days if mailed).
  3. Confirm each unit's anniversary date and rent increase license before serving.
  4. Keep a written file: base rents, computation, notice copies, service dates, license.
  5. Have a San Francisco tenant-landlord attorney review both notices before service.

If you prefer to raise only one unit

Be prepared to show a unit-specific, non-protected reason (different anniversary date, banked-increase history, or approved Rent Board petition), apply identical notice formalities, and expect closer scrutiny from SF tenant advocates and attorneys. Consult an attorney first.

SF tenants have access to free or low-cost legal help (Eviction Defense Collaborative, Rent Board counselor, Rent Board Referral Listing). An increase applied uniformly at the allowable percentage, with proper license and notice, is the approach most likely to be reviewed as reasonable and normal business practice.
