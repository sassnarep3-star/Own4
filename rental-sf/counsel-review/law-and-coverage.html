02 / Legal framework

Start with the unit—not the landlord’s size.

The first legal question is whether each dwelling is locally rent-controlled, state-cap-covered, exempt, subsidized, or governed by a contract. A 1940 building date is a useful lead, not a legal determination.

Coverage remains open. San Francisco says many residential units built on or before June 13, 1979 have rent control. A pre-June 14, 1979 unit that is not used as a single-family dwelling may qualify, but single-family/condo, substantial-rehabilitation, subsidy, and other exceptions can change the result. The reported building year and ZIP do not resolve unit classification. City coverage guide ↗

Ownership-type caveat

Some exemptions do depend on ownership—but narrowly.

California’s state-cap exemptions for certain single-family homes or condominiums have specific ownership-entity and written-notice conditions. The statutory exemption language excludes, among others, property owned by a real estate investment trust, a corporation, or an LLC with a corporate member; failure to give the required notice can defeat that exemption. State law also has a separate, fact-specific exemption for a two-unit structure where the owner occupied one unit as a principal residence at the beginning of the tenancy and continues to occupy it, subject to statutory details. None of those facts is established here. These are not blanket rules that “corporations get a different rent increase” or “individual owners are exempt.” Civil Code §1947.12 ↗ City AB 1482 guide ↗

Effective-date discipline

Use the rate on the date rent changes—not the notice date.

The Rent Board’s guidance directs landlords to use the percentage in effect when the increase takes effect. As of November 6, 2026, 1.6% is established through February 28, 2027; a later rate was not found. A notice issued before the new rate is published does not make the old rate safe for a post-February effective date. Confirm again before serving. Banking and notice guidance ↗ Current rates ↗
