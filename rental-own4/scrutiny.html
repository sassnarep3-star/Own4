Would a San Francisco tenant lawyer sign off?

SF tenants can get free legal help (Prop F right-to-counsel; Eviction Defense Collaborative; Rent Board counselors; CRD complaint process). Any increase you serve should survive a hostile-but-reasonable review. This page is that review — line by line — for the recommended 1.6% across both units, and the harder question: one unit or both?

Scope. “Sign-off” here means: lawful under the Rent Ordinance and Civ. Code, formula-based, documented, non-retaliatory, non-discriminatory — i.e., normal business practice. No increase is immune to a challenge; the standard is whether a competent tenant attorney finds a defect. This page identifies defects before a tenant does.

Raise one unit, or both? The discrimination question

Short answer: raise both.

Raising only one unit is not per se unlawful — landlords may distinguish tenancies for business reasons. But under FEHA (Gov. Code §12955), Unruh (Civ. Code §51) and the FHA (42 U.S.C. §3604), liability follows if the distinction was because of a protected characteristic, or if a neutral decision is a pretext for it. In a two-unit building, raising only Unit 1 concentrates 100% of the increase burden on the tenancy composing Tenant A, B, C → continuing Tenant A and Tenant D. A tenant lawyer would immediately ask: why only this household? If the answer is “they complained about the heater” or “she was pregnant” or “they use a voucher” — that is unlawful. Even a perfectly innocent differential (different anniversaries, different bank histories) invites a CRD complaint, a Rent Board petition, and a fact-intensive defense.

Raising both, on the same formula, on the same effective date, with identical 30-day notices, is the strongest possible posture: disparate impact on any protected class is impossible when both households move by the same formula; any court or CRD investigator sees consistent, documented, non-discriminatory practice — this is what “normal business practice” means in SF housing disputes.

If different treatment is ever necessary

  1. Identify the verifiable, non-protected business reason (e.g., documented bank history) — never complaint history, family composition, voucher status, disability, age, or national origin.
  2. Apply it consistently to any similar tenancy; keep a written policy.
  3. Itemize the basis in the notice itself (banked years), keep the rent history in the file.
  4. Have a licensed California attorney or the Rent Board counselor review the notice before service.
  5. Expect — and welcome — a tenant petition; be ready to prove every number.
